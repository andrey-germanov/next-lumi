To split expenses with roommates, agree on a method for shared costs (equal or proportional to income), decide who pays each bill, and keep a running record of who owes what so nobody has to chase anyone. Money is one of the most common sources of roommate tension, and almost all of it comes from unclear expectations rather than bad intentions. A little structure up front prevents most of the friction.

## Step 1: Decide how you will split

There are three fair models. Pick one together before anyone moves in, if you can.

- **Equal split.** Everyone pays the same share of rent and shared bills. Simple and common, and it works well when incomes and room sizes are similar.
- **Proportional to income.** Higher earners pay a larger share. Fairer when incomes differ a lot, though it requires everyone to be open about numbers.
- **Weighted by room or usage.** The larger bedroom pays more; whoever uses far more of a utility chips in extra. Useful when the space or usage is clearly unequal.

There is no single correct answer. The correct one is the split everyone agrees feels fair, agreed out loud.

## Step 2: Separate fixed shared costs from variable ones

Two kinds of expenses behave differently, so handle them differently.

- **Fixed shared costs** — rent, internet, base utilities. These are predictable, so assign each bill to one person's name and have the others transfer their share on a set day each month.
- **Variable shared costs** — groceries, household supplies, the occasional shared dinner. These are where things get messy, because they happen constantly and in small amounts. The fix is to log them as they happen rather than trying to reconstruct the month from memory.

## Step 3: Keep a running record of who owes what

The single biggest source of roommate money stress is the mental math of "wait, didn't I pay for the last one?" Keep a shared, visible record instead. Whether it is a shared note, a spreadsheet, or an app, the goal is the same: every shared expense is logged with who paid and how it splits, so settling up is a glance, not an argument.

The trick, exactly as with any [expense tracking](/blog/what-is-expense-tracking), is speed. If logging a shared grocery run takes more than a couple of seconds, people stop doing it, and the record falls apart.

## Step 4: Settle up on a schedule

Do not let balances drift for months. Pick a rhythm — weekly or on payday — and settle then. Small, frequent settle-ups keep the amounts low and the resentment lower.
