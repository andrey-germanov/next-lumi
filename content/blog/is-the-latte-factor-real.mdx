The latte factor is half true. The **math** behind it is real: small daily purchases add up and, invested over decades, can compound into a surprisingly large number. But the **story** attached to it — that your morning coffee is the reason you are not wealthy — is misleading. For most people, a few big fixed costs matter far more than any daily latte, and the coffee framing quietly points attention at the wrong place.

## Where the idea came from

The "latte factor" was popularized by financial author **David Bach**. The pitch: skip a $5 daily coffee, invest the money instead, and over 30–40 years compound growth turns it into tens or even hundreds of thousands of dollars. It became one of the most repeated pieces of personal-finance advice of the last two decades, precisely because the arithmetic is genuinely striking.

## The part that is true

Compounding is not a trick. Redirect roughly $5 a day — about $150 a month — into investments earning a long-run return, and over several decades it really can grow into a six-figure sum. Two honest lessons survive the hype:

- **Small, recurring amounts are not small over time.** A modest daily habit is really a large annual and lifetime number in disguise.
- **Automation beats willpower.** The mechanism that makes the latte factor work is not deprivation; it is consistently moving money somewhere it can grow, before you can spend it.

## The part that is wrong

Here is where the story breaks down.

**The returns are cherry-picked.** The eye-popping totals assume decades of uninterrupted, above-average returns. Use more conservative, realistic assumptions and the number shrinks a lot.

**Coffee is rarely the real leak.** For most households, the biggest expenses are housing, transport, insurance, subscriptions, and debt — not a $5 drink. You can skip every latte for a year and save less than you would by renegotiating one bill or cancelling a few [forgotten subscriptions](/blog/how-to-find-forgotten-subscriptions). Optimizing the smallest line item while ignoring the largest is backwards.

**It shames instead of informs.** "You are not rich because of coffee" turns a math point into a moral one. It ignores that many people's budgets are tight because of income and fixed costs, not indulgence — and guilt is a poor budgeting tool.

## The honest reframe

Take the useful half and drop the myth: **small recurring costs deserve attention, but only after you have looked at the big ones — and only if you actually know which is which.** That last part is the catch. Almost nobody's real spending matches their guess. People who think coffee is their problem often discover, once they track for a month, that delivery fees or subscriptions dwarf it; people who feel virtuous about skipping lattes sometimes miss a large recurring cost hiding in plain sight.

You cannot cut the right thing until you can see the whole picture. That is the real first step — not giving up coffee, but finding out where the money actually goes. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is the latte factor real or a myth?

Both. The compounding math is real — small daily amounts invested over decades can grow a lot. The myth is the implication that coffee is why people are not wealthy; for most, large fixed costs matter far more.

### Does skipping coffee actually make you rich?

Not on its own. Redirecting $5 a day into investments helps over decades, but only with steady returns and only if it is not distracting you from bigger, more impactful cuts.

### What should I cut instead of coffee?

Start by tracking for a month, then look at your largest recurring costs first — housing, transport, insurance, debt, and subscriptions. That is usually where the real money is.

### Why is the latte factor so popular if it's misleading?

Because the arithmetic is genuinely eye-catching and easy to repeat. It is a great illustration of compounding — just a poor diagnosis of most people's actual budgets.

## The takeaway

Keep the compounding lesson, skip the coffee-shaming. The smartest first move is not to quit your latte on faith — it is to track your spending, find your real biggest leaks, and fix those.
